The 60-second big picture

Think of grading school essays. The whole workflow is this analogy, made rigorous:

1 · The teacher writes the answer key and the grading guide

That's the rubric and the gold set. Only a human who understands users, the head teacher, writes them. AI never defines “good.”

↓

2 · Teaching assistants grade with the guide

The AI judges grade the same outputs using the teacher's rubric. Two TAs from different “schools” (model families) are better than one.

↓

3 · Check: do the TAs agree with the head teacher beyond lucky guessing?

That number is kappa. Raw percent-agreement lies, it doesn't subtract chance. Bar: 0.60 per construct.

↓

4 · Where they disagree, the head teacher reviews each case

Was the guide unclear (fix the guide) or did the TA err (note the bias)? That review is adjudication: and walking someone through one real case beats any table of numbers.

Core vocabulary

Twenty minutes here saves hours later. You’ll keep meeting every one of these words.

Measurement words

TermMeaning
ConstructOne thing you're measuring. 'Good' broken into small pieces you can check. Example: for alt text, 'descriptive accuracy' is one construct.
OperationalizationTurning a fuzzy idea into things you can actually see and check. Example: 'helpful' becomes 'names the main object in under 125 characters.'
RubricThe written grading guide. It lists what you're measuring and what a 0, 1, and 2 look like. Example: our alt-text rubric has three constructs: accuracy, usefulness, concision.
AnchorA real example showing what each score looks like. Keeps every grader using the same standard. Example: an accuracy 2 reads 'Golden retriever puppy lying in green grass, looking at the camera.'
Gold setThe fixed set of examples everything gets measured against. It never changes mid-eval. Example: our 12 PowerPoint images and their grades.
Ground truthThe grades that count as correct. Set by trained humans, never by AI. Example: your blind grades in Step 5.

Agreement words

TermMeaning
Blind gradingGrading without seeing anyone else's scores, so theirs can't sway yours. Example: you grade all 12 inputs before the judges run.
LLM-as-a-judgeAn AI model doing the grading so you can grade thousands of outputs. A useful tool, not the truth. Example: Judge A grades the same 12 inputs you did.
Inter-rater reliabilityHow often independent graders agree. High agreement means the test works. Example: you and Judge A agreed on 10 of 12 accuracy scores.
Cohen’s kappaA score for how much graders agree, minus the agreement you'd get by luck. 1.0 is perfect, 0 is chance. Example: our accuracy kappa was 0.71, above the 0.60 bar.
AdjudicationA human settling a disagreement by deciding what caused it: an unclear rubric or a grader mistake. Example: INPUT-06, you said 2 and Judge A said 1. Verdict: the rubric was unclear.
Criteria driftYour idea of 'good' shifting as grading teaches you what you really mean. Fine, as long as you write down each version. Example: rubric v2 after the INPUT-06 verdict.
Anthropic’s five words (used across the industry): Task: the single product behavior evaluated. Trial: one attempt at an input. Grader: whatever scores: person, script, or AI judge. Transcript / trace: the full record. Outcome: the final state. We grade outcomes, never paths.

Design principles

The principles behind the workflow.

1
Match the rigor to the risk. Tier inputs light / standard / deep. Full rigor everywhere wastes effort; light everywhere adds risk.
2
Recruit graders like the instrument they are. Qualify on practice items before grading the gold set.
3
Write “good” down before measuring. The definition of good is a document, not a vibe.
4
Keep a human in the grading loop, forever. AI judges are instruments; humans are the standard.
5
De-identify before you evaluate. Privacy is a design constraint, not cleanup.
6
Ship the standard with the enablement. A standard nobody can run is a wish.
7
Report the honest number. Better decisions from an honest 0.6 than a flattering 0.9.

THE ARTIFACT
The words, in plain English (Step 0.1). Start at the top even if you have never touched AI.

FROM ZERO
LLM (large language model): the AI that writes answers, like Copilot or ChatGPT. You give it words, it writes back words.
Model: one specific AI. A model family is a group of related models from one maker.
AI judge: an LLM given your rubric and asked to grade outputs, like a temp worker with a scoring sheet. Record the exact model (name and version): a judge you cannot name, you cannot reproduce.
Prompt: the instructions you give the AI. The judge prompt is the rubric plus the grading procedure, worded exactly the same for both judges.
Temperature: a setting that controls how random the AI's answers are. 0 means as consistent as possible. Judges run at 0 so the grades do not wobble.
Agent: an AI that takes steps instead of just writing an answer: calling tools, checking results, adapting.
Harness (scaffold): the software around the model that lets it act: feeds it inputs, runs its tool calls, collects results. When you eval "an agent," you test the model AND the harness together.
Tool call: one action the agent takes through the harness, like "look up the ticket" or "run the vision model."

THE EVAL WORDS (Anthropic's five, plus ours)
Input: what you feed the AI.
Task: one test: an input plus its success criteria.
Trial: one attempt at a task. Agents vary run to run, so one input may need several trials.
Reference output: what a correct answer contains. It proves the task is solvable.
Candidate output: the AI's actual answer. The thing you grade.
Gold set: your reference outputs. The answer key.
Construct: one quality you measure, like "descriptive accuracy." Named from user evidence, never from model features.
Rubric: the scoring sheet: each construct with anchored 2/1/0 examples.
Anchor: a concrete example of what a 2, 1, or 0 looks like. Anchors stop every grader inventing their own standard.
Grader: whatever scores an output: a person, a script (deterministic check), or an AI judge.
Blind grading: grading without seeing anyone else's scores, so they cannot pull yours.
Kappa: a number from -1 to 1 for how much two graders agree, AFTER removing agreement that could happen by luck. 1 is perfect, 0 is chance, below 0 is worse than chance.
Trust bar: the kappa you demand before trusting a judge. Default 0.60, the "substantial agreement" convention.
SHIP / HOLD: the verdict per construct. SHIP means both judges cleared the trust bar. HOLD means at least one did not, so you cannot trust that judge there yet.
Disagreement: two graders scored the same thing differently. Never averaged. Investigated.
Triage: sorting disagreements by leverage, worst first.
Adjudication: the verdict on one disagreement: whose score stands, and why.
Red-team: attacking your own rubric like a hostile reviewer, to find ambiguities before grading starts.
Bias: a judge's bad habit unrelated to quality. Position bias favors the first answer; verbosity bias favors the longest.
Trace (transcript): the full run record: steps, tool calls, model output.
Outcome: the final state when the agent is done, not the play-by-play. Grade the outcome, never the path.
Synthetic input: an input the AI generated, not one a real user produced. Cheap at scale, but its labels are guesses until a human validates them.
Dogfooding: using your own product the way a user would, to find real failures.
Criteria drift: the rubric quietly changing meaning over time. Versioning the rubric is how you manage it.
Capability eval: "what can it do?" Starts with a low pass rate. A hill to climb.
Regression eval: "does it still work?" Stays near 100%. Anything below means something broke.
Mode log: the record of which steps were human-led and which were AI-assisted. Audit evidence that a human defined ground truth.

Phase 1 · Define what “good” means

Steps 1–4 · Human-led. The most important phase.

WHO
You, human-led (AI may help draft/expand). Mode: Human+AI.
WHAT
Collect inputs from real user pain: build the task pool. Start with 12 in the pilot, grow to 20–50, then hundreds or thousands at production scale. One row per input (one row = one trial) in tasks.csv, each with a reference output.
WHERE
tasks.csv, sources in order: support tickets & failure reports, session logs, user interviews.
WHEN
~2 hours. First step of Phase 1.
WHY
An eval only measures what its inputs cover. If inputs don't look like messy reality, a high score says nothing about real UX. 24 varied inputs beat one perfect example.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Pull candidate inputs from real pain: the ticket, the session, the quote where the AI failed.
  2. Write a reference output per input. It proves the task is solvable and verifies your graders: the reference must pass every grader. Two domain experts should independently reach the same verdict on each task; if they would not, the task needs rewriting.
  3. One row = one trial. Agent outputs vary run to run, so run each input 3–5 times as separate rows (INPUT-01a, INPUT-01b) to measure how often it succeeds. What matters is the success rate, not one lucky run.
  4. Log where each input came from in Source. Real failures and manual checks beat synthetic ones: converting user-reported failures into tasks keeps the suite honest.
  5. For agentic tasks, write the expected Outcome: the final state, not the play-by-play. Grade the outcome, not the path the agent took.
  6. Stratify across input types on purpose (worked example: photos, charts, diagrams, screenshots).
  7. Balance should-do vs should-not cases (inputs where the AI must hold back).
  8. Tier every input by risk: light / standard / deep. De-identify before anything is graded.
  9. Load the workbook: model outputs → Blind Grading Sheet; reference outputs → Gold Set.
  10. Size it to the decision. 12 inputs: learn the machinery, direction only. 20–50: stable enough to act on, and the most you can hand-grade without drowning. Hundreds or thousands: tight enough for launch decisions, but only with trusted judges doing the grading. Small samples are noisy: at n=12, a kappa of 0.55 and 0.65 may be the same underlying agreement, so do not over-read small differences.

WHY

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Read each construct's definition, then its three anchors.
  2. Self-test: invent a NEW 0, 1, and 2 example per construct, not the one in the file.
  3. Note the two rules: 'Reasoning first, scores last' and the 'Unknown' out, they fight snap judgments and forced guesses. Give the AI judge the same way out an expert gets: return Unknown when it cannot tell from the output.
  4. Pick the grader type per construct. Use a deterministic check where possible (exact, cheap, reproducible), an AI judge where nuance is needed, human review sparingly as the gold standard.
  5. Grade what the agent produced, not the path it took. Checking exact tool-call sequences makes brittle tests: agents regularly find valid approaches you did not anticipate.
  6. Make graders hack-resistant. Passing must genuinely require solving the problem, not exploiting a loophole in the check.
  7. Find the red-team prompt at the bottom. You'll need it next.

Steps 5–6 · Human-only. Your grades become the reference standard.

WHO
You, the human grader. Mode: Human-only. AI must NEVER touch the matt_* columns.
WHAT
Grade all 12 outputs: 5 constructs × evidence quote + 0/1/2 score. Your grades become the reference standard.
WHERE
Blind Grading Sheet → then copy locked matt_* columns into the Gold Set tab.
WHEN
~2–3 hours, one sitting if possible (your internal standard drifts with breaks).
WHY
Someone has to define correct, and it has to be a human who understands users. The AI judges get tested on how well they match YOU, not the other way around. Grading blind matters: knowing another grader's score unconsciously pulls yours toward it.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. 0) QUALIFY FIRST: grade 3 practice items against a key, and only proceed when you agree with the key.
  2. Solo with no teammate? Make your own key: grade 3 outputs, sleep on it, then re-check each score against the anchor wording + reference outputs with fresh eyes. Disagreements with yourself = rubric problems to fix before the real round.
  3. 1) Rubric v2 side by side. Answer Key stays sealed.
  4. 2) Per output per construct: match the anchor → quote the exact evidence → reason → then score. Reasoning first, scores last.
  5. 3) Genuinely ambiguous → 'Unknown.' Forced guesses corrupt the gold set.
  6. 4) The rubric is law, not your gut. Lock each score; changes only via Step 14 with a written note.
  7. 5) Copy the 60 locked grades to Gold Set matt_* columns. THEN unseal the Answer Key.

THE ARTIFACT
The diagnosis two judges buy you:

• You vs BOTH AIs (e.g. you=0, both=2) → either you're wrong (check honestly) or both models share a blind spot.
• ONE AI vs everyone else (you=1, Judge A=1, Judge B=2) → likely that model's bias. A grader problem, not a rubric problem.
• ALL THREE differ (0, 1, 2) → the rubric anchor is probably ambiguous.
• Both AIs agree with each other but not you → re-examine YOUR grade first.

Phase 4 · Measure agreement

Steps 11–13 · The script computes; you interpret.

WHO
You run it; the script computes. Mode: Human+AI.
WHAT
compute_agreement.py → pairwise kappa per construct per pair + the full disagreement triage list.
WHERE
Terminal. It reads your Gold Set tab exported as CSV.
WHEN
~20 min.
WHY
Eyeballing 180 grades (12 outputs × 5 constructs × 3 graders) can't tell you how much graders agree, humans remember dramatic disagreements and forget quiet agreements. The script counts everything impartially.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. In Excel: File → Save As → CSV UTF-8 (.csv) → rename to gold_set.csv. Download compute_agreement.py into the same folder.
  2. No terminal? Paste the script AND the CSV into a chatbot with: 'Run this Python script on this CSV and show me the full output.' The script stays the source of truth, the chatbot is just the runner.
  3. Terminal: python3 compute_agreement.py (no terminal? paste both files into a chatbot as the runner, the script stays the source of truth).
  4. Save the output → agreement_report.txt. A result you can't re-read is a result you can't defend.
  5. Spot-check one construct by hand-counting two or three rows before trusting the table.

  judge_b  vs judge_a : kappa = +0.18  (n=12)  [LOW]
  judge_b  vs you      : kappa = +0.45  (n=12)  [LOW]
  judge_a  vs you      : kappa = +0.71  (n=12)  [OK ]

[proper_form]
  judge_b  vs judge_a : kappa = +0.73  [OK ]
  judge_b  vs you      : kappa = +0.87  [OK ]
  judge_a  vs you      : kappa = +0.87  [OK ]

Target: kappa ≥ 0.60 before trusting an automated judge.
WHO
You. Mode: Human-only, interpretation is judgment; the script can't tell you what a number means.
WHAT
Judge every kappa against the 0.60 bar, per construct per pair. Write one sentence per construct.
WHERE
agreement_report.txt, add a 'Reading' section at the top.
WHEN
~30 min.
WHY
The decision point the whole workflow builds toward: can we trust an AI judge on THIS construct? Kappa is the right number because raw percent-agreement lies, it doesn't subtract lucky agreement. (Two graders who always guess '2' agree a lot by pure chance.) One caveat: when nearly every score is the same (say, all 2s), kappa can look low even when graders agree a lot. If raw agreement is very high but kappa is low, check the score distribution before distrusting the judge.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Mark every cell: clears the bar (≥0.60) or below.
  2. One sentence per construct: 'Conciseness: both judges clear the bar (0.85, 0.67), trustworthy here.' / 'Descriptive accuracy: Judge B at 0.45, not trusted; needs triage.'
  3. Respect the small sample: n=12 is noisy. A 0.55 and a 0.65 may be the same underlying agreement. Don't over-read small differences.
  4. The 0.60 bar is OUR decision for this pilot, defensible, not a universal constant.
THE ARTIFACT
The kappa scale (Step 12):

1.0  = perfect agreement
0.80+ = near-perfect
0.60–0.80 = substantial ← OUR BAR: 0.60 (the Landis & Koch convention for 'substantial' agreement)
0.40–0.60 = moderate
below 0.40 = poor
0    = no better than chance
<0   = worse than chance (systematic disagreement, red flag)

A judge can clear on Conciseness (0.85) and fail on Descriptive accuracy (0.45). One overall average would hide that, which is why the table is per construct.
WHO
You. Mode: Human-only (deciding what a disagreement means is judgment).
WHAT
Read the triage queue, rank by leverage, pick which cases to investigate.
WHERE
agreement_report.txt, triage section.
WHEN
~20 min.
WHY
The numbers told you WHETHER there's a problem; the list tells you WHERE. Each entry: one output, one construct, all three scores side by side. The solution is never 'average them', averaging hides the reason, and the reason is the valuable part.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Pattern-match each entry: you-vs-both → your error or shared blind spot · one-vs-all → that model's bias · all-three-differ → ambiguous anchor · both-AIs-vs-you → re-examine YOUR grade first.
  2. For each failure, open the trace and ask: did the agent make a genuine mistake, or did the grader reject a valid solution? Failures should seem fair: it must be clear what the agent got wrong and why. If scores will not climb because the grader is unfair, the task is ambiguous, or the harness constrained the agent, fix the eval before blaming the agent.
  3. Rank: start with constructs that missed the kappa bar, highest leverage.
  4. Pilot rule: one deep triage, done well, beats five shallow ones. (Production: triage all of them.)
THE ARTIFACT
REAL triage entries (sample data):

INPUT-03 [descriptive_accuracy]
  judge_b : 0
  judge_a : 2
  you     : 2        → one-vs-all: likely Judge B's bias

Phase 5 · Adjudicate

Steps 14–16 · Resolve disagreements like a professional.

WHO
You. Mode: Human-only, the line no AI crosses: deciding what 'correct' means when graders conflict.
WHAT
Take your chosen disagreement and determine its cause. Document the verdict in the notes column + report.
WHERE
gold_set.csv (disputed row) + Rubric v2 (the anchor in question).
WHEN
~45 min. Narrate your thinking OUT LOUD, record it. This is the Monday centerpiece.
WHY
Every disagreement has a cause, and the fix depends on the cause. Misdiagnosing wastes the fix: rewriting a good rubric because one model was sloppy, or excusing a bad rubric because 'the AI just made a mistake.'

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Record yourself with Loom, Zoom, or your phone, talk through Q1 → Q2 → verdict out loud. The recording is the deliverable.
  2. Q1: Could two careful, reasonable humans disagree here? Read the anchor literally. If its words permit both readings → RUBRIC AMBIGUITY → Step 15.
  3. Q2: If the rubric is clear, which grader misread it, and HOW? Name it: 'Judge B scored accuracy 0 citing long alt text, but length is conciseness.' 'Judge B was wrong' is not a diagnosis.
  4. Check YOURSELF: re-derive your grade from the anchor as if you'd never seen the number. If it changes, say so openly.
  5. Write the verdict: Verdict: [rubric ambiguity | grader error: who, what | broken input | wrong reference | my grade was wrong | policy call | insufficient evidence] → Action: […].
THE ARTIFACT
The adjudication decision tree:

Disagreement in hand
  └─ Q1: Could two reasonable humans disagree here?
       ├─ YES → rubric ambiguity → Step 15: minimal anchor edit
       └─ NO → Q2: which grader misread what?
            ├─ grader error → name it → bias note (don't touch rubric)
            ├─ my grade was wrong → correct openly, log it
            ├─ broken input / wrong reference → fix the fixture
            └─ genuinely debatable → policy call: escalate, don't grade through it
WHO
You. Mode: Human-only, changing the definition of 'good' is the most human step of all.
WHAT
If rubric ambiguity: the minimal anchor edit that resolves it → v3 + changelog. If grader error: bias note, NO rubric edit.
WHERE
Rubric section v2 → v3; or judge_bias_notes.md.
WHEN
~30 min.
WHY
A rubric is a living instrument. Every ambiguity you fix makes the next hundred grading decisions more consistent. Versioned openly = rigor (criteria drift, managed). Hidden drift = the actual danger.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Minimal edit: one anchor's wording, one example, one clarified boundary. Rewriting the whole rubric throws away what was working.
  2. Changelog at top: 'Changed <construct> anchor <0/1/2>: <old> → <new>. Because: <triaged case IMG__>.'
  3. Re-grade affected items if the meaning shifted (expected, not cheating).
  4. Grader error? Do NOT edit the rubric, file the bias: which judge, which construct, what pattern, what it means at scale.
  5. Log it: 'Rubric v3: Human-only (I authored the change; the disagreement is cited).'
THE ARTIFACT
Changelog format (top of the Rubric section):

Changelog (v2 → v3)
• Changed descriptive_accuracy anchor 1: 'mentions the chart type'
  → 'names the chart type AND one data trend it shows.'
  Because: triaged IMG-05 (Step 14), 'bar chart' alone earned a 1 from one judge, 2 from another.

One anchor. One example. One boundary. Minimal matters.
WHO
Script computes; you judge whether the fix worked. Mode: Human+AI.
WHAT
Re-grade affected items under v3, re-run the script, confirm the numbers moved the right direction.
WHERE
gold_set.csv → terminal → agreement_report.txt ('Round 2' section).
WHEN
~45 min.
WHY
An edit you don't verify is a hope, not a fix. Rubric change → re-grade → higher kappa: that's evidence the instrument improved. This measure-fix-remeasure cycle is the heartbeat of all evaluation work.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Update the Judge Prompt to v2 with the new anchor; re-grade affected items (you + both judges).
  2. Re-run python3 compute_agreement.py; append under '## Round 2 (rubric v3)'.
  3. Write the delta in plain words: 'Descriptive accuracy kappa (you vs Judge B): 0.42 → 0.71 after clarifying the visible-vs-inferred boundary.'
  4. No improvement? Say so and triage again, also a finding. The ambiguity may run deeper than one anchor.
THE ARTIFACT

Round 1 (rubric v2)                    Round 2 (rubric v3)

        ▲ after clarifying the visible-vs-inferred boundary

Phase 6 · Stress-test for bias

Steps 17–18 · Trust, but verify.

WHO
AI executes; you read. Mode: AI-only execution, human read.
WHAT
Show a judge the same two outputs in both orders (A-then-B, then B-then-A) in fresh sessions. Do scores flip?
WHERE
Fresh judge sessions, no file changes. Result → judge_bias_notes.md.
WHEN
~30 min.
WHY
Language models tend to prefer the first-presented option, the primacy effect. If a judge's scores change when only the order changed, it's grading position, not quality. Cheap test; expensive bias.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Pick two gold-set outputs of clearly different quality (e.g. strong IMG01, flawed IMG03).
  2. Session 1: judge prompt, A first, B second, 'score both 0/1/2 on descriptive accuracy.' Record.
  3. Session 2 (fresh): same prompt, B first, A second. Record.
  4. Same output, different scores → position bias. Note direction (favors first? last?) and size.
  5. Mitigation: randomize presentation order, or better, score pointwise (like our rubric) instead of comparatively.
THE ARTIFACT
The experiment:

Session 1 (fresh):  [A strong] then [B flawed]  →  A=2, B=0
Session 2 (fresh):  [B flawed] then [A strong]  →  B=1, A=2  ← scores moved!

Verdict: position bias, the judge scored B higher just for going first.
Log: 'Position-bias test, <date>: <judge>, order-flip changed descriptive_accuracy by 1 → favors-first bias; mitigate by randomizing order.'
WHO
AI executes; you read. Mode: AI-only execution, human read.
WHAT
Take one good concise output; make a padded twin (identical facts, twice the filler). Does the judge score the padded one higher?
WHERE
Fresh judge session. Result → judge_bias_notes.md.
WHEN
~30 min.
WHY
Judges notoriously reward longer answers, fluent padding feels thorough. A judge that prefers bloat would green-light exactly the signal-vs-noise failures users hate.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Take an alt text that scored well on conciseness. Write its padded twin: same facts + 'This image shows…' preamble + restated details. Change NO facts.
  2. Fresh session: judge scores both on conciseness.
  3. Padded ≥ original → verbosity bias. Record the size of the effect.
  4. Mitigation: explicit length guidance in the anchor (ours already has it: no preamble, every word earns its place) or length-normalized comparisons.
THE ARTIFACT
The experiment:

ORIGINAL (conciseness 2):
'Bar chart: Q3 revenue rose 12%, driven by enterprise.'

PADDED TWIN (same facts):
'This image shows a bar chart. The bar chart displays Q3 revenue. Revenue rose 12% in Q3, and this increase was driven by enterprise customers…'

Judge scores padded ≥ original → verbosity bias confirmed.

Phase 7 · Cover more ground, then scale

Steps 19–22 · Turn the exercise into a system.

WHO
AI generates; YOU validate. Mode: Human+AI, validation strictly human-only.
WHAT
Generate additional inputs for thin slices; hand-validate a sample before ANY of it counts.
WHERE
synthetic_tasks.csv (same columns as tasks.csv) + validated_by_human column.
WHEN
After the gold set exists. Never before.
WHY
Hand-writing inputs is slow and coverage needs hundreds. AI generation is the only way to scale. But generated inputs have generated labels, and an AI's guess is not ground truth. Unvalidated synthetic data measures the generator's imagination, not user reality.

HOW (offline: do it in your own files)

  1. Use a DIFFERENT AI session from your judges, so the generator's habits and phrasing don't leak into grading.
  2. Find thin slices: which scenario types have the fewest inputs in tasks.csv?
  3. Prompt an AI (different session from your judges): 'Generate 10 inputs in the <thin slice> style of these 2 examples, each with reference alt text.'
  4. Hand-validate ≥5 or 20% (whichever is larger): realistic? Would this image appear on a slide? Reference alt text correct against the image YOURSELF? Fix, delete, or rewrite fiction.
  5. Mark validated_by_human yes/no. Only 'yes' rows count toward coverage.
  6. De-identify seeds before sharing. Log it.
THE ARTIFACT
The rule that governs this step:

'Synthetic data EXPANDS coverage; it NEVER becomes ground truth without human validation.'

Validation checklist per sampled row:
☐ Realistic: could this input actually occur?
☐ Reference correct: checked against the input myself
☐ De-identified: no names, emails, identifiers
☐ Marked: validated_by_human = yes/no

A synthetic input that could never occur is worse than no input: it teaches the eval to care about fiction.
